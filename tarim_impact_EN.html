ABSTRACT

This work presents a hypothesis in which the impact of an iron-nickel body ~300 km in diameter on the Tarim Basin (39°N, 83°E) in the Late Pleistocene (~12,800 years ago) is considered the primary cause of the formation of Earth's major surface features: lithospheric plates, mountain systems, continental shelves, oceanic ridges, as well as a number of geophysical, climatic, and biological phenomena.

The hypothesis builds on the morphological analysis of the Tarim Basin as a mega-impact structure (Ernstson, Claudin, 2020), on geochemical markers at the Younger Dryas boundary (osmium and platinum anomalies), on the universality of mythological accounts of a planetary catastrophe, and on a set of empirical observations amenable to an alternative interpretation within a single impact event.

This work does not claim final precision of all quantitative estimates. Its goal is to demonstrate the internal consistency of a scenario in which one planetary event unifies a wide range of observed phenomena, and to formulate testable predictions. Where the proposed interpretation conflicts with the standard model, the conflict is stated explicitly as an open question requiring further investigation.

1. STATEMENT OF THE PROBLEM AND METHODOLOGY

1.1. Methodology

This work presents the hypothesis in the form of an alternative scientific paradigm. By paradigm we mean a system of premises within which all observed phenomena are viewed as consequences of a single primary event. A similar method was applied by Wegener in formulating continental drift: a unified mechanism was opposed to a multitude of disparate explanations.

Presentation principle: for each key phenomenon we indicate how it is interpreted within the impact paradigm and explicitly note where this interpretation conflicts with the standard model. The goal is not to conceal conflicts, but to make them subjects of further research.

1.2. Key Premise

The main premise of the hypothesis: the impact of an iron-nickel body ~300 km in diameter on Earth ~12,800 years ago became the primary cause of the formation of most observable relief elements and a number of geophysical phenomena. Consequences are derived from this premise, some of which are verifiable by independent methods.

1.3. Companion Visualization

An interactive three-dimensional visualization of the scenario (tarim_earth_3d.html) and its video version accompany this work. The scenario reproduces the sequence of sections 2-11: a rewind to a hypothetical pre-impact appearance of the planet (an illustrative texture with a single landmass centered on the epicenter), entry of the body at 37° against the rotation, an impact with a melt dome, radial crust divergence and plate drift, convergence of the wavefront at Chicxulub (~20 minutes, §7), a dust winter, the Younger Dryas cooling, a meltwater pulse, and the transition to the present configuration.

The visualization is an illustrative artifact, not a numerical model. Time scales are compressed unevenly; the scenario constants, however, match the computed values of this work: the year counter is set to 12,800 cal BP — a rounding of the Allerod/Younger Dryas boundary interval (YD onset 12,870 ± 30 cal BP; Cheng et al., 2020) at which the geochemical markers are recorded (§13; the GISP2 platinum peak is 12,833-12,738 cal BP against annually layered ice: Petaev et al., 2013); the pre-impact axial tilt is ~24° per astronomical theory (§10.3); the instantaneous tilt impulse is 0.125° (computed in §10); the subsequent change to the present 23.44° is shown as a smooth drift. The plate-divergence model (§4.5) does not set the calendar date: the impulse phase completes within weeks. Throughout the text the event is denoted as "~12,800 years ago".

3. IMPACT PHYSICS AND PENETRATION SCENARIO

3.1. Impact Energy

E = (1/2) m v^2 = (1/2) × 1.113×10^20 × (3.0×10^4)^2 = 5.0 × 10^28 J

Comparison:

  • Chicxulub (~66 Ma): ~4.2 × 10^23 J
  • Tarim impact: ~119,000 × Chicxulub
  • Earth's gravitational binding: ~2.24 × 10^32 J
  • Tarim impact: ~0.022% of binding energy

Enormous energy, yet a negligible fraction of Earth's binding energy. The planet was not destroyed, but its upper layers were reshaped.

3.2. Penetration Scenario

A 300-km body entering at 30 km/s at 37° does not explode at the surface (as smaller bodies do). Its size is comparable to the lithosphere thickness (~100 km). The body punches through the lithosphere and descends into the mantle.

Phases: (a) ATMOSPHERIC ENTRY (seconds). The atmosphere cannot decelerate a 300-km body: the column density of air (~10 t/m2) is negligible compared to the body's surface density (the mass per cross-sectional area: ~1.6 × 10^9 kg/m2 ~ 1.6 × 10^6 t/m2). The body enters at full cosmic velocity. [Comparison: the Hoba meteorite (60 t) was decelerated because its surface density (~8 t/m2) is comparable to the atmosphere. For a 300-km body this is impossible — see §15.]

(b) LITHOSPHERE BREAKTHROUGH (~10-20 seconds). The body punches through the lithosphere, splitting it into plates. Shock waves propagate radially from the impact point, forming a system of fractures. The breakthrough scenario has direct analogues in the mechanics of elongated-body impact: high-velocity long bodies tunnel into a target, pushing material ahead, rather than exploding at the surface (Orphal, 2006; O'Keefe, Ahrens, 1982). At an oblique entry, a substantial fraction of the energy is directed horizontally — into the shear fracture of the lithosphere — rather than into vertical destruction of the surface; the exact energy partition is subject to numerical verification (§18).

(c) MANTLE DESCENT (minutes). The body sinks to a depth comparable to its size. For a 300-km impact at 30 km/s the estimated penetration depth is ~150-800 km. At this depth, temperature and pressure cause the body to begin melting.

(d) DISSOLUTION AND REDISTRIBUTION (thousands of years). Molten iron is denser than the silicate mantle and gradually sinks further (the mechanics of iron-mantle mixing: Dahl, Stevenson, 2010). Complete dissolution and mixing require extended time; the fate of the body's material is an open question (see §17).

3.3. Core Displacement

The body's momentum is transferred to the core: p = m v = 1.113×10^20 × 30000 = 3.34 × 10^24 kg·m/s Core mass: 1.94 × 10^24 kg Core velocity: v = p / m_core ~ 1.7 m/s Core displacement: ~850 m

Core displacement creates a moment-of-inertia asymmetry that may initiate true polar wander (see §10).

4. FORMATION OF LITHOSPHERIC PLATES

4.1. Scenario

Within the impact paradigm, lithospheric plates formed as a result of the splitting of a unified lithosphere by the 300-km body. The pre-impact lithosphere is comparable to the crust of modern Mars — a single shell without active segmentation into plates. The crust fractured radially from the impact point; the resulting plates began to move over the asthenosphere under the action of:

  • Initial impulse (divergence from the epicenter)
  • Earth's rotation (Coriolis deflection)
  • Antipodal shock-wave focusing (return toward the epicenter)

After the initial outward flight, shock waves that had traversed the planet reached the antipode and reflected, creating a return impulse. This may explain why present-day plate motion directions are largely oriented toward the Tarim Basin: plates that initially flew outward subsequently received a reverse impulse.

4.2. Front Geometry: the Tangency Circle and Rotational Asymmetry

The first (strongest) shock impulse propagates radially from the epicenter through a near-surface waveguide confined to the lithosphere. On a spherical planet the geometry of such a front is described by the epicentral distance psi (angular distance from the epicenter):

  • The psi = 90° arc has length (pi/2)R ≈ 10,000 km.
  • The front covers the area A(psi) = 2 pi R^2 (1 - cos psi); at

psi = 90° the front coincides with a great circle — the epicenter's "horizon", covering exactly half the planet's surface (~2.55 × 10^8 km2).

  • Up to the 90° circle the front expands and the energy density in it

decays as ~1/sin psi; beyond the 90° circle the front converges toward the antipode — up to antipodal focusing (§7).

The 90° circle is the only line along which a radial front is tangent to the surface: before it, the front strikes the crust from outside; beyond it, the front travels along the surface, and the vertical component of the stress field acts downward. Within the model this transition is treated as a change of the loading regime: radial compression along the layer gives way to loading applied to the base of the upper crust (see 4.3).

Rotational asymmetry. The source, the medium, and the crustal structures co-rotate, so the front arrival times in the eastern and western sectors coincide to within second-order corrections ((v_rot/c)^2 ~ 1%, where v_rot = 465 m/s is the equatorial rotation velocity and c ~ 4 km/s is the wave velocity in the lithospheric waveguide). The loading asymmetry requires a strict reference frame. The quantities c - v_rot (~3.5 km/s) and c + v_rot (~4.5 km/s) are the front velocities relative to surface points in the inertial frame; for waves carried by the rotating crust, the loading duration of a point in the co-moving frame is the same in both sectors, and the rotational asymmetry reduces to second-order corrections (~1%). The first-order asymmetry in the model is set not by the rotation but by the directed impulse of the impactor (entry against rotation, westward; p = 3.3 × 10^24 kg·m/s, §3.3), by the shift of the effective source during penetration, and by the Coriolis curving of the diverging material: inertial-circle radii r = v/(2 omega sin phi) range from tens to thousands of kilometers, so panel trajectories are not straight radial lines but curved arcs with decaying amplitude. The ability of passing surface fronts to activate faults at distances of thousands of kilometers is established empirically — the dynamic triggering of earthquakes (Wu et al., 2011; Prejean, Hill, 2011).

4.3. Superficial Crust Spall and Deep-Sea Trenches

During the unloading of a strong shock impulse at a free surface, tensile stresses arise that exceed the tensile strength of rocks (the spallation mechanism; Melosh, 1989). The upper-bound estimate of the spall thickness h ~ c dt/2 with an impactor coupling time D/v ~ 10 s and c ~ 5.5-6.5 km/s gives ~25-35 km — the order of the upper third of the continental crust. Within the model it is assumed that the actual spall involved a near-surface layer ~12 km thick (the upper third of a 30-40 km crust), which fits within the upper-bound estimate; the exact value is subject to numerical verification (§18).

The steps of crustal thickness are the trace of the spall:

continental crust 30-45 km transitional basins 15-20 km oceanic crust 6-10 km

The sharp thickness boundary (the spall step) in the western Pacific coincides with the deep-sea trench system (Kuril-Kamchatka, Japan, Izu-Bonin, Mariana, Philippines). Here the continental waveguide loses its propagation channel: surface waves convert into body waves and descend — by analogy with the known blockage of the Lg phase at the transition to thin crust (Aki, Richards, 2002); the load is directed into the base of the upper crust and completes its detachment. The greatest depths are confined to the longest spall fronts (the Mariana Trench, ~10,900 m).

The sequence of trench arcs (Aleutian -> Kuril-Kamchatka -> Japan -> Izu-Bonin-Mariana) is interpreted as a staged spall: a series of successive fronts with decreasing load, in which each successive stage is longer than the previous one, and the planet's continuous rotation turns the front azimuth. The arcs retain the shape of great-circle arcs, which is testable (§18).

4.4. The Peel Line and the Re-Accretion Line

From the asymmetry (4.2) it follows that the eastern (Pacific) sector underwent the spall first and to the greatest extent; antipodal focusing (§7; antipode 39°S, 97°W) additionally amplified precisely this sector. The detached panels (the present North and South America) received the westward-directed impulse of the impactor and drifted across the Pacific, shaving off the remaining near-surface layer: the trace of this shave is the linear volcanic chains and the young floor of the Pacific. The mechanical prerequisite of the drift is a global low-viscosity layer at the top of the asthenosphere (the LVZ): its viscosity decreases further under shear, providing the decoupling of the lithosphere from the mantle (Doglioni et al., 2015; Carcaterra, Doglioni, 2018). The total path of the panels under decaying curvilinear drift (4.2) exceeds the net displacement by an order of magnitude: for South America the total path is estimated at ~3.5 × 10^4 km with a net displacement of ~5 × 10^3 km.

The two boundaries of the American panels are fundamentally different:

  • The western margin (the Cordilleras, the Andes) is the peel line:

a great-circle arc lying at epicentral distances of ~95-105° from the epicenter (the western margin of North America ~100°, equatorial Atlantic ~104°). On a flat map such an arc looks like an oblique slash, because it is a projection of a geodesic. Along the peel line the mountain systems are raised — by the same mechanism as the wound edge (§5).

  • The eastern (Atlantic) margin is the re-accretion line: the place

where the westward-carried panels met the fragments of the unified continent that drifted west slowly (the western sector received a smaller spall, 4.2-4.3). The suture of this meeting is the Mid-Atlantic Ridge; its shape repeats the line of convergence of the two panel families.

The western sector of Eurasia shows a braking regime: stresses accumulated from the Urals (~23° from the epicenter) westward, and the crust deformed in a fold-and-fault manner — producing a series of rifts (the Black Sea, the Mediterranean) and the ragged outlines of Europe. Arabia and Africa detached along boundaries of weakness and moved toward the American panels, closing the re-accretion belt.

4.5. Impulse Phase of the Divergence and Motion Maintenance

Panel velocity in the impulse phase decays exponentially: v(t) = v0 × exp(-t / tau)

v0 = 500 m/s (initial divergence velocity) tau ≈ 20 hours (the time of active loading by the front)

The total panel path under this law: S = v0 × tau = 500 m/s × 7.2 × 10^4 s ≈ 3.6 × 10^4 km, consistent with the geometric estimate of the total path of the South America panel (§4.4): ~3.5 × 10^4 km. During the first relaxation time a panel covers 1 - 1/e ≈ 63% of its total path; during two — ~86%.

Energy check: the kinetic energy of the lithosphere (mass ~1.7 × 10^23 kg) at v0 = 500 m/s is ~2.1 × 10^28 J — about 40% of the impact energy (5 × 10^28 J); the rest is spent on fracturing, deformation, and heating. Notably higher initial velocities (kilometers per second and above) exceed the energy budget.

The present plate velocity (~8 cm/yr) in the model is not the tail of the impulse exponential (it burns out within weeks) but a separate motion-maintenance process.

A note on motion maintenance. Present-day absolute plate motions are westward-polarized: a systematic westward component is seen in GPS solutions, and plate boundaries are directionally asymmetric — subduction zones with westward-dipping slabs are on average steeper and accompanied by back-arc extension, whereas eastward-dipping ones are shallow and lack back-arc basins (Doglioni et al., 2015). The westward drift of the lithosphere as an observed quantity and its tidal interpretation have a long history: the first quantitative statement was Bostrom (1971); modern constraints from global mantle flow models and asthenospheric viscosity were given by Conrad, Behn (2010). Within the model, this polarization inherits the direction of the impactor's impulse (§2). A candidate mechanism for maintaining the residual westward drift after the decay of the impulse component is the tidal "ratchet": the daily tidal oscillation, made asymmetric by the Moon's revolution, in combination with the nonlinear rheology of the LVZ is rectified into a steady westward shear (Carcaterra, Doglioni, 2018; Nesi et al., 2023). If a tidal component is present, the exponential law describes only the impulse part of the motion, and the observed velocity has a non-zero asymptotic level — this is a testable consequence distinguishing the sum of the two components from a pure exponential. The quantitative separation of the components and the magnitude of the westward lithosphere rotation (literature estimates diverge, ~0.1-1.2°/Ma) are recorded as debated points.

4.6. Conflict with the Standard Model

The standard model (plate tectonics, Wegener 1912; Hess 1962; Vine, Matthews 1963) views lithospheric plates as existing for billions of years, with motion sustained by mantle convection. Marine magnetic stripes are interpreted as a record of seafloor spreading and geomagnetic reversals (Vine-Matthews-Morley, 1963).

Within the impact paradigm the present plate configuration and motion are viewed as the result of a recent fracture. Magnetic stripes in this interpretation may reflect simultaneous magnetization of cooling basalts along fracture lines, or damping oscillations of the perturbed core, rather than a million-year record of reversals.

Independent modeling support: numerical experiments show that a major impact can initiate subduction and the transition from a unified lithosphere to plate tectonics (O'Neill et al., 2017; the "hot post-impact core-mantle boundary -> mantle plumes -> subduction initiation" mechanism: Yuan et al., 2024). Within the paradigm this mechanism operates at the recent boundary: the 300-km impactor is the trigger of the plate regime.

Open question (see §17): how the observed consistency of magnetic stripes with the geomagnetic polarity timescale (GPTS) fits within the impact model.

An additional conflict: subsections 4.2-4.4 presuppose the existence of a tangency circle as the boundary of the loading-regime change and a recent (~12,800 yr) spall of the upper crust. In linear elasticity surface waves circle the planet along complete loops with no sharp boundary at the 90° circle, and the biostratigraphy of the Atlantic floor indicates an ocean many times older. Both conflicts are converted into testable predictions (§18).

5. MOUNTAIN SYSTEMS AS THE RAISED EDGE OF A WOUND

Mountains surrounding the Tarim Basin are viewed as the raised edge of the "wound" created by lithosphere penetration:

Himalayas (Everest, 8,849 m) ~1,500 km from epicenter Karakoram (K2, 8,611 m) ~700 km Pamir (Ismoil Somoni, 7,495 m) ~1,150 km Tien Shan (Pobeda, 7,439 m) ~370 km Kunlun (7,167 m) ~340 km

All of Earth's highest mountain systems lie within ~1,500 km of the Tarim Basin. This is taken as an argument that their uplift is a consequence of a single process rather than independent collisions.

The standard model explains these mountains by the India-Eurasia plate collision (initial contact ~65-59 Ma in modern reviews; Wang et al., 2025; the hard-collision stage ~50-45 Ma). Within the impact paradigm the collision may be reinterpreted as a consequence of the initial plate divergence from the impact and their subsequent encounter.

Open question (§17): thermochronological data indicate multi-stage uplift. Within the impact paradigm this requires reinterpretation, either as an artifact of dating methods, or as evidence of an additional (pre-impact) uplift phase.

6. SEDIMENTARY ROCKS AT GREAT ALTITUDES AND DEPTHS

6.1. Marine Fossils on Mountain Tops

Marine sedimentary rocks with fossils (crinoids, trilobites, brachiopods of Ordovician age) are found on the summit of Everest (8,849 m) and other Himalayan peaks.

Standard interpretation: the Tethys Ocean existed here for hundreds of millions of years; collision lifted the seafloor to 8 km altitude.

Alternative interpretation (impact paradigm): marine sediments at the surface were uplifted to mountain height by the explosive raising of the wound's edge. An impact of 5×10^28 J has sufficient energy to eject material to heights of several kilometers.

6.2. Marine Sediments at Great Depths (Kola Superdeep Borehole)

The Kola Superdeep Borehole (12,262 m) found biogenic rocks and fossilized microorganisms at depths below 6 km.

Standard interpretation: ancient marine sediments buried tectonically and metamorphosed over billions of years.

Alternative interpretation: surface sediments forced downward by the shock wave during lithosphere fracture. Within the impact paradigm this would explain the presence of biogenic material at 6+ km depth without invoking a billion-year sedimentation history.

Open question (§17): degree of rock metamorphism at depth. The standard model links metamorphism to prolonged residence at high temperature and pressure. Within the impact paradigm, metamorphism may result from impact heating.

7. ANTIPODAL EFFECTS

7.1. Mechanism

Shock waves from a 300-km impact propagate through the planet and focus at the antipode. Antipodal seismic-wave focusing is a real physical phenomenon observed instrumentally (USArray: amplification of surface waves up to an order of magnitude at the antipode; Retailleau et al., 2014; numerical confirmation: Meschede et al., 2011). Antipodal waves also serve as a working planetary-probing tool: they have been used to detect a partially softened zone at the surface of the inner core (Tsuboi, Butler, 2026).

A precedent for an antipodal pair is known outside our subject: for the Permian-Triassic boundary, a link has been proposed between an impact basin in Wilkes Land (East Antarctica; a gravity anomaly in GRACE data) and the Siberian Traps (von Frese et al., 2009). Regardless of the status of that particular hypothesis, the mechanism itself — "an impact at one point — a tectono-magmatic response at the antipode" — is present in the literature. A similar trigger link between an impact and major magmatism was discussed for the Cretaceous-Paleogene boundary (Richards et al., 2015).

Antipodal focusing is a working tool of modern seismology: the inner core focuses earthquake waves to their antipode, which is used to probe the deepest layers of the core (Phạm, Tkalčić, 2023). A planetary precedent is equally established: the "hilly and lineated terrain" at the antipode of the Caloris basin (Mercury) is explained by focusing of impact-generated seismic waves (Lü et al., 2011).

7.2. Chicxulub as a Possible Antipodal Structure

The geometric antipode of Tarim: 39°S, 97°W; Chicxulub: 21.3°N, 89.7°W — the angular distance between them is ~61°. Earth's rotation during PKP-wave transit (~20 min) gives ~5° of longitude and contributes little; for waves carried by the rotating planet, no first-order rotation-induced displacement arises (§4.2). Focusing displacements of tens of degrees require an oblique source — the wave-beam tilt at a 37° entry and the planet's volumetric heterogeneity. The quantitative focal position is a task for 3D wave-propagation modeling (test 12); without it, the focal latitude is not computed strictly.

Focus estimate: ~21°N, ~92°W (agreement with Chicxulub in longitude within ~5°; in latitude — approximate). Within the impact paradigm, the Chicxulub structure could have been modified by antipodal wave focusing from the Tarim impact; the crater itself formed as a separate, earlier event (~66 Ma, §7.3).

Testable prediction: shock minerals in Chicxulub rocks should show isotropic (non-directed) orientation if deformation is caused by wave focusing from all sides rather than a direct top-down strike.

7.3. Conflict with the Standard Model

The standard model treats Chicxulub as an independent impact ~66 Ma, dated by radioisotopic methods (⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar). Within the impact paradigm the radioisotopic dates may be reinterpreted as artifacts of impact heating (see §14).

8. SOUTH ATLANTIC MAGNETIC ANOMALY (SAA)

The South Atlantic Magnetic Anomaly is a zone of weakened geomagnetic field over the South Atlantic. The field drops from ~45,000 nT to ~22,000 nT.

Within the impact paradigm, SAA is explained by displacement of the liquid core toward the antipode of the impact point upon penetration of the Fe-Ni body. Core displacement disrupts the symmetry of the geodynamo, weakening the dipole field over this zone.

The standard model explains SAA by a gyre (large-scale flow) in the liquid core (Finlay, Aubert, Gillet, 2016, Nature Communications) that carries reversed flux to the pole. This does not require an impact.

Open question: whether the impact contribution to SAA can be quantitatively separated from the gyre contribution.

9. LARGE MANTLE STRUCTURES (LLSVP)

Seismic tomography reveals two giant structures in the lower mantle — large low-shear-velocity provinces (LLSVP) — beneath Africa and the Pacific Ocean.

Within the impact paradigm:

  • The impact was westward-directed → mantle material was displaced

in two directions: (1) WESTWARD (direct impulse) → African LLSVP (1,500 km) (2) EASTWARD (recoil) → Pacific LLSVP (700-900 km)

  • The African lobe is LARGER because the direct impulse exceeds

the recoil.

  • Beneath Asia (the impact site) — a COLD zone: material was expelled.
  • Separation between LLSVPs ~155° (not exactly 180°) is consistent

with the asymmetry of a 37° oblique strike.

The standard model views LLSVPs as long-lived thermochemical piles formed by mantle convection over hundreds of millions of years.

Independent support for an impact origin of basal mantle anomalies appeared in 2023: numerical modeling showed that large impacts can form stable provinces of seismically slow material at the core-mantle boundary, analogous to the observed LLSVPs (Yuan et al., 2023, Nature 623). A scale caveat: Yuan et al. model the Moon-forming impact (a Mars-sized body), orders of magnitude larger; the mechanism "impact material reaches the core-mantle boundary and forms slow provinces" is demonstrated for that scale, and its transfer to a smaller one requires a separate numerical calculation (test 11). Within the impact paradigm the 300-km impactor is a candidate source of the observed LLSVP pair, and its asymmetry (see above) follows from the directed 37° entry.

Further support: a 2025 seismological analysis shows that the two LLVPs have distinct compositions and evolutionary histories (Panton et al., 2025). Within the impact paradigm this difference is natural: the direct westward impulse and the eastward recoil form bodies with different properties; the standard model attributes the difference to different histories of subducted material.

Open question (§17): the age of LLSVPs. Standard estimates are hundreds of millions of years. Within the impact paradigm, their age may be significantly younger if formed by impact-driven mass redistribution.

10. ROTATION AXIS AND TRUE POLAR WANDER

10.1. Scenario within the Impact Paradigm

The impact transfers angular momentum and displaces the core. Mass asymmetry initiates true polar wander (TPW) — a shift of the rotation axis relative to the principal axes of inertia of the planet; large TPW episodes have also been reconstructed for earlier epochs (Kirschvink et al., 1997).

Within the impact paradigm, the orientation of the planet's figure relative to the rotation axis may have substantially changed after the impact: for an observer on a plate, the latitude of the site changed — the height of the midday sun and the appearance of the starry sky. This is reflected in mythological accounts of "sky tilting" (Gong Gong in China: "the sky fell to the northwest"; Phaethon in Greece: "the Earth tilted").

Instantaneous tilt from angular momentum transfer: L_meteorite / L_Earth = 0.00218 delta_theta = arctan(0.00218) = 0.125°

Further axis shift may have occurred through TPW initiated by core displacement and mantle mass redistribution (plates, LLSVPs).

Trigger and executor. The impactor does not "push" the axis: its role is to alter the mass distribution (the ~8.6 km effective core displacement, §10.2; the plates; the LLSVPs). The rest is the planet's own work: gravitational-viscous relaxation rotates the figure under the new principal axis of inertia. Three processes must be distinguished:

  • precession — the conical motion of the axis in space under the

gravitational torque of the Sun and Moon on the equatorial bulge; period ~25,771 years; it existed before the impact and is not created by it;

  • true polar wander — a single damped (tau ~ 1,200 years, §10.2)

rotation of the figure relative to the unchanged axis; this is what the impact could initiate;

  • a change of the tilt to the ecliptic (obliquity) — the

Milankovitch cycle (22.1°-24.5°, 41,000 years); unattainable by internal processes (the 0.125° bound, §10.2). The general frame: an external torque is required to change the tilt; internal mass redistribution shifts the pole over the surface. Independent data on other planets support this frame. Uranus's tilt (98°) is explained only by external mechanisms — a series of giant impacts or a spin-orbit resonance with a massive satellite or disk (Boué, Laskar, 2010; Morbidelli et al., 2012); a planet is not tilted by internal redistribution. The stability of Earth's obliquity within the 22.1°-24.5° corridor is provided by the stabilizing torque of the Moon (Laskar et al., 1993): without it, Earth's precession constant would be small, and planetary resonances would drive the tilt chaotically by tens of degrees.

10.2. The "188 Times More Mass" Mechanical Bound and the Path Around It

The direct objection to an impact-induced tilt reads: to gyroscope- rotate the axis by 23.44°, an angular momentum L = L_Earth × sin(23.44°) ≈ 2.3 × 10^33 kg·m2/s is required — ~188 times the impactor's moment; a body 188 times more massive would be needed. This bound is correct but applies only to the direct rotation of a rigid gyroscope — a change in the direction of the angular momentum vector.

The Earth is not a rigid gyroscope but a deformable body with a small dynamical ellipticity (C - A)/C ≈ 3.2 × 10^-3. Reorienting the FIGURE of the planet relative to the unchanged angular-momentum vector (true polar wander) requires no angular momentum at all: it requires only a change of the inertia tensor (Gold, 1955; Spada et al., 1992; Greff- Lefftz, Legros, 1999):

dI_required = (C - A) × tan(23.44°) ≈ 1.1 × 10^35 kg·m2

The mass perturbation required is orders of magnitude below "188 masses": ~10^21-10^22 kg at a lever arm of thousands of kilometers. The impact supplies such a redistribution through two mechanisms:

(a) Core displacement. The initial displacement is ~850 m; the quasi-static effective displacement in the perturbed mantle is ~8.6 km (the inertia-tensor requirement of 1.1 × 10^35 kg·m2, computed below). A displacement of the 1.94 × 10^24 kg core by ~8.6 km shifts the mantle's center of mass by ~4 km and creates an inertia-tensor perturbation of order 10^35 kg·m2 — the same order as the required 1.1 × 10^35.

(b) Asymmetric mantle structures. The observed LLSVP pair (African ~1,500 km versus Pacific ~700-900 km; §9) provides an additional inertia-tensor imbalance.

The consistency is meaningful: the effective core displacement of 8.6 km corresponds to the displacement required for the full 23.44° figure rotation via the inertia tensor: d_req = (C - A)·tan(23.44°)/(2 m_core R) ≈ 4-9 km depending on the lever-arm geometry.

The geodetic constraint. The observed present-day polar wander is ~0.003"/yr (on the order of 1° per million years). At tau ~ 1,200 years, the initial rate of the figure rotation (23.44° / 1,200 yr ~ 70"/yr) burns down to the observed level within ~5 tau (~6,000 years): the residual rate by the present is ~0.003"/yr, comparable to the geodetic background, while the rate in the first centuries after the event is orders of magnitude higher — a testable consequence for paleomagnetic and archaeomagnetic records (§10.4).

Level-by-level decomposition and the angular momentum conservation bound. The impactor's angular momentum is compared not only with the moment of the planet as a whole:

  • the spin angular momentum of the inner core is ~ (4.4-5.4) ×

10^30 kg·m2/s: the impactor's momentum (1.26 × 10^31 kg·m2/s) exceeds it by a factor of 2-3 — the impact is capable of fully re-orienting the inner core's rotation axis, and the weak gravitational and electromagnetic coupling allows such a tilt to persist for a long time;

  • the spin angular momentum of the core as a whole is ~6.6 ×

10^32 kg·m2/s: the impactor can tilt the core's spin by only ~1.1°;

  • the planet as a whole: 0.125° (computed above).

A consequence of angular momentum conservation: any internal redistribution (core-mantle exchange) cannot shift the total axis by more than L_impactor / L_Earth = 0.125°; a large tilt is unattainable by internal flows. The only mechanism of a large displacement without changing the direction of the L vector is the rotation of the figure via the inertia tensor (the TPW path described above). Rejected alternatives: the gravitational torque of a flying-by fragment gives an effect 7-8 orders of magnitude below what is required; a spin-orbit resonance would require the Earth's precession constant to change by roughly a factor of two; perturbation of the lunar orbit by ejecta and atmospheric-oceanic torques are negligible.

Observational context. Present-day polar wander is measured geodetically; the standard model closes the 20th-century velocity budget with a combination of glacial isostatic adjustment and mantle convection (Ghelichkhan et al., 2025). Within the paradigm, the observed rate is a superposition of this background and the residual post-impact wander; separating the contributions is a testable task (§10.4). An additional budget uncertainty: different versions of global plate reconstructions yield azimuthal disagreements of modeled wander trajectories exceeding 70° (Calvelage et al., 2025). Paleorecords document the phenomenon itself at large scale: the Pacific plate's paleomagnetic record for the interval from 56 to 48 Ma shows a large latitudinal displacement of hotspots — an episode of substantial true polar wander (Woodworth et al., 2026).

Core mobility is independently confirmed: repeated seismic waveforms record changes in the differential rotation of the inner core (Wang et al., 2024). The standard interpretation is a 70-year oscillation; what matters for the paradigm is the very fact that the core exhibits rapid rotation changes on decadal scales.

The picture has been refined by the 2025-2026 work. From long earthquake sequences and the USArray network, a stable pattern of a multidecadal oscillation has been recovered: the inner core rotated faster than the mantle in the 1980s, near zero by 2000, and slower after 2010, with no short bursts (Wu et al., 2026). A direct analysis of repeating earthquakes showed that not only the rotation rate but also the near-surface part of the inner core changes — viscous deformation has been detected at its boundary (Vidale et al., 2025). What matters for the paradigm: the inner core is a dynamic, deformable body responding to torques from the mantle and the outer core; the same mobility makes both the preservation of a residual impulse imprint and its partial overprinting possible.

An independent fingerprint: an ~8.5-year oscillation in the Earth's rotation yields a static tilt of ~0.17° between the rotation axes of the inner core and the mantle (An et al., 2023). The standard interpretation is growth misalignment. The order of magnitude matches the computed impulse (0.125°); within the paradigm this is a possible residual trace of the inner-core spin re-oriented by the impact (coupling of a tilted inner core to polar motion: Dumberry, Bloxham, 2002).

Thus the mechanical objection "188 times more mass is needed" is removed: it applies to the direct rotation, whose contribution is only 0.125° (~0.5% of the total angle); the remaining ~99.5% is TPW, which requires no angular momentum. The conflict with the astronomical record (10.3) remains, but it moves from the category of mechanical impossibility to that of an empirically unsettled question.

10.3. Conflict with the Standard Model — STATED EXPLICITLY

The current obliquity (axial tilt to the ecliptic) = 23.44°. Standard astronomy (Laskar solutions, La2004/La2010) treats obliquity as the Milankovitch cycle (22.1°-24.5°, period 41,000 years), traced in marine sediments over hundreds of millions of years (Zachos et al., 2001). By these data, ~12,800 years ago obliquity was ~24°.

Within the impact paradigm this requires reinterpretation: (a) Either the Milankovitch cycles in δ18O have a different cause, not axial tilt. (b) Or the pre-impact obliquity was substantially different, and the observed δ18O cycles are a superposition of climatic and orbital effects.

THIS IS AN OPEN QUESTION, acknowledged within the paradigm. The author does not claim that the Milankovitch cycles are false; he notes that their interpretation as a purely orbital phenomenon may be incomplete if a major impact changed the axis orientation.

Distinguishing the paths resolves part of the conflict. True polar wander shifts the pole over the planet's surface without changing the tilt of the axis to the orbital plane: the figure rotates about the unchanged L vector (§10.2). The astronomical obliquity record and the mythological accounts of the sky and the Sun shifting refer to different quantities: the former fixes the tilt to the ecliptic, the latter — a possible shift of the geographic pole. The conflict of this section concerns only the strong version (the impact changed the obliquity by tens of degrees), which the paradigm does not require: the TPW path describes a pole shift at unchanged obliquity.

10.4. Testable Prediction

If the axis shifted ~12,800 years ago, paleomagnetic evidence of an abrupt pole-position change on this interval should exist. Archaeomagnetic and paleomagnetic data (GEOMAGIAIA50, sediment records) may contain this signal.

11.1. Immediate Effects

  • Mega-tsunami
  • Global fires from incandescent ejecta
  • Partial atmosphere loss in the impact zone

11.2. Medium-Term (years-centuries)

  • Impact winter (stratospheric dust and fire soot, reduced

insolation; for the K-Pg, models yield 15,000 Tg of soot and near-total surface darkness --- Bardeen et al., 2017)

  • Possible climate-zone shifts (TPW)
  • Water-mass redistribution

A quantitative atmospheric budget for the Tarim scenario has not been computed: the masses of dust, soot, water vapor, and aerosols, the injection height, and the atmospheric residence time are the subject of numerical modeling (tests 11-12). The K-Pg estimate of 15,000 Tg of soot is a scale anchor for an event orders of magnitude larger; linear extrapolation is incorrect. Verification — comparison against ice cores, marine and lacustrine sediments, and speleothems (§18).

11.3. Younger Dryas (~12,800 cal BP)

Sharp cooling at the Younger Dryas boundary. Winter temperatures up to 24°C colder with warm summer periods. Extreme seasonality.

Standard model: disruption of thermohaline circulation, freshwater discharge. A volcanic alternative (the Laacher See eruption) is discussed but links only part of the cooling to the YD (Baldini et al., 2018). A stratigraphic test of this alternative: in the high-resolution Körslättamossen record (southernmost Sweden) the Laacher See Tephra (~13,006 cal BP) lies 6-10 cm below a discrete Younger Dryas boundary horizon enriched in platinum-group elements and containing microspherules — the eruption precedes the fallout by roughly 150 years and does not coincide with the YD boundary (Moore et al., 2026). A regional marker in High Asia: reprocessing of 10Be exposure ages of moraines on the Tibetan Plateau identifies three glaciations within the Younger Dryas (around 12.5, 11.9 and 11.5 ka BP); the temperature reductions agree with global records (Cui, Li, 2026). Within the impact paradigm — a consequence of impact winter and possible climate-zone restructuring.

12. BIOLOGICAL CONSEQUENCES

12.1. Megafauna Extinction

Extinction of continental mammoths, giant sloths, cave bears, and saber-toothed cats within the Pleistocene/Holocene boundary interval. Eurasian species show genetic bottlenecks; African species do not. The chronology is not strictly synchronous and is recorded honestly: the main extinction of the Australian megafauna precedes the boundary (~40-50 ka) and lies outside the damage zone — the paradigm does not explain it, and it remains with the competing causes (climate, humans); some mammoth populations persisted for a long time (Wrangel Island — until ~4 ka). A testable consequence of the paradigm: a concentration of regional population-extinction dates near the event interval within the zone of ballistic and thermal damage (§18).

The zone of ballistic and thermal damage with a characteristic radius of ~6,000 km covers Eastern Siberia (~4,000-6,000 km from the epicenter) — the region of mass burials of mammoth fauna in permafrost. Individual finds with poorly digested stomach contents are interpreted within the model as a consequence of rapid (hours-to-days) cooling after the impact. A strict taphonomic verification of this feature (the share of finds with preserved contents among all finds) is a necessary condition for using it as an argument. In the standard model, the extinction is explained by a combination of climatic and anthropogenic causes without instantaneous freezing.

12.2. Human Change

The emergence of agriculture (Neolithic Revolution) in several centers roughly simultaneously ~10,000-12,000 years ago. If the climatic setting changed abruptly, the need for agriculture could have arisen simultaneously in several regions.

13. GEOCHEMICAL MARKERS

13.1. Osmium Isotopes (Os)

A conference abstract by Beets et al. (2008, AGU Fall Meeting; no extended peer-reviewed paper exists): in a terrigenous Usselo-horizon section 187Os/188Os = 0.53 within an interval of values >1.1, corresponding to ~57% extraterrestrial osmium in the sediments. The source status is recorded honestly. A direct peer-reviewed test in marine sections returned a negative result: across the Bolling-Allerod/Younger Dryas transition 187Os/188Os stays within a few percent of present-day seawater, with no extraterrestrial input (Paquay et al., 2009); the reply points to unanalyzed magnetic fractions enriched in iridium (Bunch et al., 2010). The osmium argument remains unresolved: terrigenous and marine sections give different answers; the discriminating test is systematic Os mapping of the boundary by section type (test 7). Climatic calibration of the scale: in a 20-kyr record of the seawater osmium isotope composition from the Okinawa Trough, shifts amount to only ~5-8% — during cold intervals (including the Younger Dryas boundary) values shift toward less radiogenic compositions, which the authors attribute to water-mass change and reduced continental flux (Lim et al., 2026); global records give the same order: shifts toward ~8.1 in 187Os/186Os (equivalent to ~0.97 in 187Os/188Os) over late-glacial intervals of elevated aridity (Oxburgh, 1998; the ratio conventions differ and are comparable only through conversion). The climatic component is an order of magnitude smaller than the claimed terrigenous signal (0.53 vs >1.1).

13.2. Platinum Anomaly (Pt)

Petaev et al. (2013, PNAS): a platinum peak in the GISP2 ice core (Greenland) at the Younger Dryas boundary. Pt concentration rises roughly 100-fold above background (~30 pg/g). The Pt/Ir ratio is superchondritic — characteristic of iron meteorites, not chondrites or comets. Moore et al. (2017) confirmed the spread of the Pt anomaly to 11 widely separated sequences across North America. A separate network of sites has been documented in the Western Alps (Viso Massif, Italy and France): a platinum anomaly at every studied section, elevated Pt/Pd, and an AMS date of 12,816 ±44 cal BP synchronous with the Younger Dryas boundary (Mahaney et al., 2024) — an intermediate link between the North American sites and Abu Hureyra (13.3).

Within the impact paradigm, the Pt anomaly is the geochemical fingerprint of the body's Fe-Ni composition (84% Fe, 16% Ni).

13.3. Impact Glass and Tektites

Impact glass (melted rock) is a standard impact marker. In the Tarim Basin area, morphological analysis indicates melted impact structures at the surface. A systematic petrographic study of this material is a necessary next step.

The Australasian tektite strewn field (~790 ka, the largest and youngest field of impact glass on Earth) has no confirmed source crater: a candidate — a buried structure beneath the Bolaven volcanic field, Laos (Sieh et al., 2020) — has been proposed but not confirmed. This is a scientific puzzle showing that major impact events can leave widespread tektites without an easily identifiable crater. The dispute is active: a new layered-only zone in southwestern Hainan has been used to localize the source in the Yinggehai-Song Hong basin (Pan et al., 2026); the Bolaven team disputed this localization in a published comment with the authors' reply (Geology, 54 (7), e601-e602). The impact origin of the glass itself is independently confirmed: coesite has been found inside near-field microtektites — a high-pressure phase recording shock transformation of target quartz prior to melting (Folco et al., 2025).

A marker of a similar nature is impact diamonds — graphite-to-diamond phases formed under shock compression (the Popigai structure). Their search in the rocks of the Tarim Basin is a separate testable task (§18).

The direct impact line of the YD boundary is also traced west of the epicenter. The systematic base of YD-boundary markers (magnetic spherules, melts, soot, shocked quartz) was laid by the interdisciplinary 2007-2013 program (Firestone et al., 2007; Bunch et al., 2012; Wittke et al., 2013; review: Sweatman, 2021). At the settlement of Abu Hureyra (Syria, ~3,900 km to the west-southwest), melts with a melting temperature above 2,200 °C, shock-fractured quartz, and spherules were found in the layers of the Younger Dryas onset (Moore et al., 2020; 2023). Geographically this corresponds to the western divergence sector, where, per 4.2, a weaker but traceable signature is expected. Independently, the YD-onset layer with pyrogenic markers and cosmic-impact traces has been recorded in South America as well — the Pilauco site, Patagonia (Pino et al., 2019): the evidence covers both panels of the split unified continent. A geochemical re-evaluation of the Hall's Cave section (Texas) supports an impact rather than a volcanic origin of the YD-boundary event (Young, 2025).

13.4. PGE Spectrum as a Discriminating Test

Testable prediction: a PGE-spectrum measurement (Pt/Ir, Pt/Pd, Os/Ir, Ru/Ir) in Tarim Basin sediments at ~12,800-year depth should show superchondritic ratios characteristic of Fe-Ni material (cf. PGE spectra of iron meteorites: Campbell, Humayun, 2005).

Honest caveat: the origin of the Pt peak has two named alternatives. The volcanic one has been formulated on the basis of modern volcanic-emission analogues (Green et al., 2025); the local meteorite one — a noncataclysmic entry of the Cape York iron meteorite (Boslough, 2013), answered by a reply defending the global character of the anomaly (Petaev et al., 2013b). The question of the Pt peak's origin remains open.

13.5. Thickness of the Global Boundary Layer

Classic cratering events (K-Pg) form a global layer centimeters to decimeters thick. For the Tarim event the model predicts a significantly thinner layer for two reasons:

  • Retention of the impactor mass in sediments drops sharply with

increasing impact velocity: at velocities above ~25 km/s only a small fraction of the mass is retained globally, and the source mass required for a given iridium anomaly grows rapidly with velocity (Jeffers et al., 2001).

  • Low-angle impacts vaporize orders of magnitude more rock mass

than vertical ones (Schultz, 1987), diluting the impactor signature and suppressing shock minerals in the impact area.

The observed state — thin layers: the claimed terrigenous Os signal (13.1, contested status) and Pt ~100× above background (13.2) — corresponds precisely to this regime: the high velocity (30 km/s) and oblique entry (37°) predict a weak but globally traceable layer, which is what is observed. Recent experiments and numerical studies of oblique impacts document a sharp dependence of ejecta on the entry angle and its downrange directionality (Ye et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2025). The absence of a thick iridium horizon at the Younger Dryas boundary is thus consistent with the model rather than contradicting it. At the same time, the absolute mass of the impactor (1.1 × 10^20 kg) is such that even a small retained fraction forms a measurable horizon. A strict quantitative estimate of the retained fraction for the scenario (30 km/s, 37°) requires an ejecta calculation; the present argument matches the orders of magnitude but does not replace numerical modeling.

14. ALTERNATIVE INTERPRETATION OF GEOLOGICAL DATES

14.1. The Problem

Radioisotopic methods (⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar, K-Ar, U-Pb) and the geomagnetic polarity timescale (GPTS) assign ages from millions to billions of years to mountains, oceanic crust, craters, and sediments. Within the impact paradigm these ages require reinterpretation if most observable structures formed ~12,800 years ago.

14.2. Possible Distortion Mechanisms

Within the impact paradigm we propose considering:

(a) Impact resetting of isotopic clocks. An impact with energy 5×10^28 J heats rocks to thousands of degrees over a large area. Heating resets radioisotopic clocks (element diffusion, argon loss). Reheated rocks can yield a "false" age reflecting the last heating event, not the time of primary formation. A hard consequence bounding the mechanism: with a FULL reset, all clocks would read ~12,800 years — the observed ages of tens to hundreds of millions of years exclude a full reset for the bulk of the rocks. Two testable variants remain: partial resetting with measurable consequences (systematic age underestimation, discordance between systems with different closure temperatures), or heating localized near the impact. "Million-year" ages as heating artifacts are possible only with partial resetting and must show a measurable correlation with the degree of overheating — testable (test 13).

(b) Sediment mixing. An impact fracturing the lithosphere to 12 km depth mixes sediments of different ages. The Kola borehole found biogenic rocks at 6+ km — standard model: ancient sediments; impact paradigm: surface sediments forced down by the impact.

(c) Magnetic stripes as a record of a single event. If oceanic crust formed along fracture lines ~12,800 years ago, magnetic stripes may reflect not a million-year record of reversals, but the spatial structure of magnetization as basalts cooled along fractures.

(d) Perturbation of atmospheric 14C. The release of dust, carbon dioxide, and vapors during the impact can abruptly change the atmospheric radiocarbon concentration, shifting radiocarbon dates of the event boundary. Testable consequence: a sharp disturbance of the 14C curve in the Younger Dryas boundary layers. A methodological precedent: subfossil pines from the southern French Alps record a sharp Δ14C excursion of ~30‰ at 14,300 cal yr BP, dated to within 1-2 years by wiggle-matching (Bard et al., 2026). Sharp 14C excursions in the Late Glacial are real and precisely datable — hence the testable consequence is realizable on existing cores and rings. An independent annual-scale tool for the same interval is the varve chronology of Lake Kasplya on the Russian Plain: 4,745 ±325 annual layers over ~12.1-7.4 ka BP, agreeing with the radiocarbon age-depth model within 10% (Shasherina et al., 2026).

(e) Geochemical redistribution. Massive release of mantle material and the transport of melts during the lithosphere fracture change the chemical and isotopic background of rocks, affecting the potassium-argon, uranium-lead, and rubidium-strontium systems.

14.3. Honest Acknowledgment

These mechanisms are HYPOTHETICAL. They are unproven and require quantitative development. Standard dating methods have undergone extensive cross-validation (radioisotopes ↔ paleomagnetism ↔ astrochronology). Within the impact paradigm it is acknowledged that the reinterpretation of all dates is the greatest challenge.

THIS IS AN OPEN QUESTION. The author does not claim all dates are false. He notes that in the case of a major impact, dating methods may yield systematically distorted results, and this requires study.

15. SUPPORTING ARGUMENTS

15.1. The Hoba Meteorite (Namibia)

The Hoba meteorite is the largest intact meteorite on Earth (~60 tons, Fe-Ni). It has no impact crater.

Standard explanation: low entry angle + flat shape + low entry velocity → atmospheric deceleration to terminal velocity → soft landing without explosion.

Within the impact paradigm Hoba is considered a possible fragment of a larger body (a piece of the Tarim impactor) that broke off during atmospheric entry and landed in Africa. The absence of a crater is explained by deceleration of the fragment.

Note: for a 300-km body atmospheric deceleration is impossible (surface density ~1.6×10^9 kg/m2 ~ 1.6×10^6 t/m2 vs atmosphere ~10 t/m2). The main body would enter at full velocity. But small fragments (meters to tens of meters) detached from the main body can be decelerated by the atmosphere and fall "softly," like Hoba.

A limitation is recorded honestly: the radiometric estimate of the terrestrial age of Hoba (~80 ka based on 59Ni; McCorkell et al., 1968) exceeds the event dating by an order of magnitude. Within the model this requires either an earlier separation of the fragment from the parent body in space, or a revision of the estimate; the question remains open.

15.2. The Eye of the Sahara (Richat Structure, Mauritania)

A concentric structure ~40 km in diameter, visually resembling a crater. Standard model: a geological dome formed by a plume during the breakup of Pangaea (>100 Ma), followed by differential erosion.

Within the impact paradigm Richat is viewed as a possible consequence of the impact process — mantle-material ejection forming a concentric structure. The central core of Richat consists of magmatic rocks (kimberlite, carbonatite) of deep mantle origin, consistent with mantle ejection.

Note: shocked quartz and impact glass have not been found in Richat rocks. Within the impact paradigm this may be explained by Richat being not the impact point, but a zone of remote response (antipodal or regional deformation).

15.3. Seismic Anomaly beneath the Tarim

Sun et al. (2022): a flat-lying high-velocity layer beneath the Tarim. Standard interpretation: subducted craton lithosphere. Impact interpretation: a buried body or impact-compression zone.

Independent 2024 tomography records a pronounced high-velocity anomaly in the upper mantle beneath the Tarim (Dou et al., 2024): the anomaly's existence is confirmed by two independent groups; only its interpretation differs (a cold cratonic root in the standard model, a buried body in the paradigm). Discriminating test — Vp/Vs ratio and density (§18).

15.4. Qualitative Identifications in the Literature

The impact interpretation of the Tarim Basin has been qualitatively voiced in sources of different status (each status is stated honestly; none of these claims constitutes a diagnostic confirmation — modern criteria for confirming an impact origin require shock-metamorphic structures and/or an extraterrestrial geochemical signature: Cavosie et al., 2026):

(Ernstson, Claudin, 2020 — a non-peer-reviewed web publication; its authors themselves note that the shape may also be explained by tectonics).

  • A qualitative comparison of the Tarim Basin, the Black Sea and

Common features of these basins: thin crust with an elevated Moho boundary, thick undisturbed horizontal sediments, absence of earthquakes inside the basins amid active surrounding tectonics, and compression of the surroundings directed away from the basins.

16.5. Myths of the Golden Age

The cycle of Hesiod ("the land itself bore fruit all year round"), the Satya Yuga of the Vedas, the Sumerian Dilmun ("neither sickness nor old age"), the image of Eden — describe a world with an even climate and end with a catastrophe (a flood or fire from the sky). In Plato (Timaeus, Critias), the destruction of the prime island is dated to 9,000 years before Solon — around 9,600 BCE (~11,600 years ago), close to the event dating (~12,800 years ago, §1.3). Within the impact paradigm, the motif of an even climate is a memory of the pre-impact climatic regime, and the shared ending is a memory of the event. The physical interpretation of the pre-impact axial tilt remains an open question (§17); this work does not attribute the axial tilt to the impact. A material correlate of the motif is the warm Allerod interstadial (~13,900-12,900 cal BP) immediately before the event: the flourishing of Abu Hureyra prior to the YD boundary fire (§13.3).

An archaeological anchor — Göbekli Tepe (southeastern Turkey; construction begun at the YD boundary): the interpretation of the carved symbols of the "Vulture Stone" as a date stamp of ~10,950 ± 250 BCE and the builders' special interest in the Taurid meteor stream are consistent with an event at the YD boundary (Sweatman, Tsikritsis, 2017). The distance from the epicenter is ~3,800 km to the west-southwest — the same western sector where Abu Hureyra lies (13.3). The reading of the carvings is debated; independently of it, the monument itself is dated to the onset of the YD.

17. OPEN QUESTIONS AND CONFLICTS WITH THE STANDARD MODEL

This section records the honest boundaries of the hypothesis. Acknowledged open questions:

(a) AXIAL TILT. The Milankovitch cycles in δ18O and the Laskar solutions indicate that obliquity is a 41,000-year cycle traced over hundreds of millions of years, ~24° at 12 ka. Within the impact paradigm it is required to explain how the impact-driven axis shift reconciles with these data. Possible paths: reinterpretation of δ18O cycles, or superposition of effects. The mechanical objection ("188 times more mass is needed") applies only to the direct gyroscope rotation; the TPW pathway requires only an inertia-tensor change of ~1.1 × 10^35 kg·m2, achievable at the scale of the event (10.2). The conflict with the δ18O record remains empirical, not mechanical.

(b) DATING. Radioisotopic methods and GPTS assign ages of millions to billions of years to rocks, mountains, and crust. Within the impact paradigm a mechanism for systematic distortion of these ages by impact heating is required. This is hypothetical and needs quantitative development.

(c) MAGNETIC STRIPES. The standard model (Vine-Matthews-Morley) is confirmed by three independent methods. Within the impact paradigm an alternative stripe-formation mechanism is required. A hard numerical edge of the conflict: the mean age of the preserved oceanic crust is 64.2 Ma, the maximum ~180 Ma (Seton et al., 2020) — three orders of magnitude above the model crust age of ~12,800 years; this is assigned to the testable predictions (§18).

(d) GRAVITY ANOMALY. If a buried body of 10^20 kg is present in the mantle beneath the Tarim, a positive gravity anomaly of significant amplitude is expected. The observed Bouguer anomaly of the Tarim is negative (~-100 to -250 mGal), corresponding to a typical sedimentary basin. Within the impact paradigm the fate of the body must be explained: complete dissolution, global redistribution, or another scenario. A possible path: the body dissolved and redistributed spherically symmetrically (yielding no local anomaly), but the mechanism for such redistribution over 12,800 years requires study.

(e) IMPACT FREQUENCY. Estimates of impact rates for 300-km bodies yield intervals far exceeding the age of Earth. Within the impact paradigm the source of the body must be explained: a destroyed planet, a protoplanet-core fragment, a captured body. A candidate reservoir is the disrupted cometary nucleus of the Taurid complex: the coherent-catastrophism model predicts a series of impacts from a single breakup (Napier, 2010; Asher et al., 1994). Observational constraints: the progenitor of the resonant swarm is estimated as a body of a few to a few tens of kilometers, comparable to 2P/Encke, with no unnoticed kilometer-class members (Li et al., 2025); a reanalysis of the 2022 ZTF survey estimates roughly 10² Tunguska-sized and 10³ Chelyabinsk-sized objects within it, with the caveat that the estimate rests on an assumption about the shared orbital distribution not yet verified by independent data (Ye et al., 2026). A 300-km body is not supplied by the present-day Taurid reservoir — this mismatch is recorded honestly: a larger progenitor is required (the core of a differentiated protoplanet, §2). Empirical context on frequency: confirmed Holocene craters were unknown until recently, but their discovery continues — Jinlin in Guangdong Province (Chen et al., 2025), the largest confirmed Holocene crater (820-900 m, younger than 10,000 years); Hailin in northeast China (Yin et al., 2024) is a unique ridge-hosted structure whose age was not determined in the discovery paper. Holocene impacts are real and until recently were systematically missed.

(f) LLSVP AGE. Standard estimates are hundreds of millions of years. Within the impact paradigm reinterpretation is required.

(g) IMPACTOR IDENTITY. Alternative models (a high-velocity cometary one, ~60-75 km/s, porous body) reproduce qualitatively similar geometry of large basins (Burgener, 2015). Discrimination between the models — by geochemistry (superchondritic Pt/Ir and the Os signature indicate an Fe-Ni composition) and by shock evidence; quantitative modeling of both scenarios is required.

These questions are NOT resolved in this work. They are framed as a program for further research. Honesty demands acknowledging: the impact paradigm is in conflict with the standard model on several points, and resolving these conflicts is a condition of its acceptance.

18. TESTABLE PREDICTIONS

For each test, not only the expected result but also the result that would refute the hypothesis is fixed in advance. Tests 11-12 (numerical models) are a necessary condition for checking the central physical scenario, not an optional long-term addition.

18.1. High Priority (testable on existing data)

1. Shock minerals at Chicxulub — isotropic orientation (reanalysis

of IODP Expedition 364 cores). Refutation: a systematically directed orientation compatible only with a direct strike.

2. PGE spectrum in Tarim Basin sediments at ~12,800-year depth —

superchondritic Pt/Ir, Pt/Pd characteristic of Fe-Ni. Refutation: only chondritic or terrestrial ratios without superchondritic-signature horizons.

3. Impact glass and shock minerals in Tarim Basin rocks —

systematic petrographic study, including a search for impact diamonds (13.3). Refutation: no diagnostic features in any of the studied cores.

4. Seismic anomaly beneath the Tarim — Vp/Vs measurement to

distinguish an Fe-Ni body from subducted lithosphere. Refutation: Vp/Vs and density unambiguously matching cold lithosphere.

5. Geometric test of the peel line: approximation of the western

margins of North and South America by great-circle arcs with the pole at the Tarim Basin; the root-mean-square deviation must be systematically smaller than for the eastern (Atlantic) margins; the analysis covers the entire planetary surface, without cherry-picking. Refutation: equal or worse approximation quality for the western margins.

6. Spall-step test: using a global crustal model (Laske et al.,

  1. — confinement of deep-sea trenches to crustal-thickness

steps and Moho-depth inflections along arcs close to the psi = 90° circle; numerical modeling of a strong impulse in a spherical shell (loading-regime change beyond the 90° circle). Refutation: no statistical association of trenches with crustal-thickness steps.

18.2. Medium Priority

7. Os-isotope mapping of the Younger Dryas boundary by section

type (terrigenous vs marine; the status of the Beets 2008 / Paquay 2009 dispute — 13.1). Refutation: no extraterrestrial input in the terrigenous sections either, with a validated methodology.

8. Archaeomagnetic data at ~12,800 BP — search for a signal of

abrupt pole shift. Refutation: continuity of the virtual geomagnetic pole trajectory across the event interval.

9. Gravity anomaly — search for a possible distributed signal

from dissolved body material. Refutation: absence of the expected distributed signal for the given mass.

10. Analysis of impact glass in the Tarim area (if found).

Refutation: glass composition compatible only with volcanic or sedimentary melting, without shock features.

18.3. Numerical Models (a first-priority condition for checking the

scenario)

11. SPH modeling of a 300-km impact — lithosphere fracturing into

plates, mountain uplift, mantle penetration; the thickness of the superficial spall (4.3); the fate of the impactor and the ejecta distribution. Refutation: the model shows that at 30 km/s and 37°, mantle penetration and the upper-crust spall do not occur.

12. 3D modeling of antipodal seismic-wave focusing, including the

focal position (7.2). Refutation: the focusing amplitude at Chicxulub is below shock-metamorphism thresholds.

13. Testing the partial impact reset of geochronological systems

(14.2(a): a full reset is excluded). Refutation: systems with different closure temperatures show a consistent age underestimation within the impact zone.

Data status (for tests 1, 3, 8). Summary reviews of the Chicxulub drilling programs have been published (Urrutia-Fucugauchi et al., 2025; Gulick et al., 2026), but directed measurements of shock-mineral orientation are absent from them — test 1 remains open. An independent material for such a re-analysis is shock-deformed age-reset zircon (Zhao et al., 2025). For ultra-deep drilling in the Tarim Basin, the first vertical seismic profiling data have been published (Wang et al., 2026); petrographic analysis of deep cores — none (test 3). Systematic archaeomagnetic data for the ~12,800 BP interval are absent (test 8).

19. CONCLUSION

The Tarim Impact Hypothesis proposes to view the impact of an Fe-Ni body ~300 km on the Tarim Basin ~12,800 years ago as the primary cause of the formation of most observable Earth surface features and a number of geophysical, climatic, and biological phenomena.

The hypothesis rests on:

  • The morphology of the Tarim Basin as a mega-impact structure

(Ernstson, Claudin, 2020)

  • Geochemical markers at the Younger Dryas boundary (Os, Pt)
  • The universality of catastrophe myths
  • An alternative interpretation of several geological observations

The hypothesis is in conflict with the standard model on several points (axial tilt, dating, magnetic stripes, gravity, impact frequency). These conflicts are stated honestly as open questions.

The goal of this work is not to declare the standard model false, but to show that the impact paradigm provides an internally consistent explanation for a wide range of phenomena, and to formulate testable predictions that allow the paradigms to be distinguished empirically.

The burden of proof rests on the proposer. This work formulates a verification program and honestly acknowledges its limitations.
